▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · SWEBOK · what design means
GROUNDING · SWEBOK v4 · SOFTWARE DESIGN KA (CH.3)

What "design" actually means — the textbook answer

Before the how, be clear on the what. SWEBOK — the profession's body of knowledge — defines software design as taking your requirements, needs and concerns and turning them into, in its words, "implementable design specifications." In plain terms: a blueprint complete enough that someone can build from it.

And it says design happens in three stages, zooming from the whole system down to the inside of one module:

STAGE 1

Architectural

The whole system and how it meets its environment — the major parts and the system-wide decisions (cost, performance, security).

STAGE 2 · OUTWARD-FACING

High-level design

Each major component's role and its interface — detailed enough that others can use it without reading its code.

STAGE 3 · INWARD-FACING

Detailed design

Inside each module: its algorithms, data access, and data structures — detailed enough to actually write the code.

Our three levels map straight onto this: Architecture = Stage 1 · Detailed design (Level 2 today) = Stages 2–3 together · UX/UI = SWEBOK's User-Centered design method — we make it its own level because your project has a real interface a person touches.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Two ideas everywhere
THE TWO IDEAS UNDER ALL THREE LEVELS

Break the system into parts, then hide each part's mess

Modularity

Split into parts

Divide the system into modules — small, named pieces with one clear job each. A team of four can only build what's been split into parts they can each own.

Abstraction

Hide the details

Each module shows a simple interface and hides its inner workings. You use "send a reminder" without caring how it's sent. Change the inside freely — the outside stays the same.

Everything today — cohesion, coupling, architecture styles, class design, MVC — is just these two ideas applied at different sizes.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Cohesion
DESIGN QUALITY · 1 OF 2

Cohesion: does one module do exactly one job?

Cohesion is how focused a module is. High cohesion = everything inside belongs together. Low cohesion = a "junk drawer" class doing five unrelated things.

LOW COHESION

class StudyMateManager

Sends reminders, formats dates, talks to the database, draws the screen, and checks passwords — one giant class that does everything.

Why it hurts

Touch one feature and you risk breaking four others. Nobody on the team can own it.

HIGH COHESION

one job per module

ReminderService · TaskRepository · TaskListScreen — each name tells you its single responsibility.

Why it helps

You can find, test, and change one thing without reading the whole system.

Quick test: can you name a module in one short phrase without saying "and"? If you need "and", it's doing too much.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Coupling
DESIGN QUALITY · 2 OF 2

Coupling: how tangled are two modules?

Coupling is how much one module depends on the insides of another. Low coupling = modules talk through a small, stable interface — change one, the other doesn't notice.

High coupling (avoid)

The screen reaches directly into the database's tables. Rename a column → the screen breaks. Everything is wired to everything.

Low coupling (aim for)

The screen calls TaskRepository.getTasks() and doesn't care where the data lives. Swap the database → the screen never changes.

You will never hit zero coupling — modules must talk. The goal is few connections, through clear interfaces.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · The one rule
THE SINGLE SENTENCE TO REMEMBER THIS WEEK

High cohesion, low coupling

Each module does one job well (high cohesion) and depends on as little as possible of any other module (low coupling).

How to spot it at any level

Point at any part — a whole service, a class, or a screen — and ask two questions: "one job?" (cohesion) and "how many other parts break if I change this?" (coupling).

The trap the AI Critique will test

"Put all the logic in one big class; high coupling is good." Both halves are wrong — and you'll spot exactly why in the last 10 minutes.

This is the vocabulary graders and the oral exam will use. If your design has one god-class, you'll be asked to defend it — and you won't be able to.

STEP 1–2 · FROM MODULES TO THE WHOLE SYSTEM

Architecture = the decisions you can't easily undo later

Cohesion and coupling shape one module. Architecture is the high-level structure of the whole system — decided early, expensive to reverse once the code exists.

01

What are the pieces?

How many major parts does the system split into?

02

How do they talk?

Direct calls? A network request? A queue?

03

A model (Week 4) is a map of one feature. Architecture is the map of the whole city that feature lives in.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · SWEBOK · style families
SWEBOK v4 · WHY THERE ARE SO MANY "STYLES"

Each style answers a different question — so you use several at once

A common confusion: "is my system Layered, or MVC, or Client-Server?" — as if you must choose one. You don't. SWEBOK sorts styles into families, and each family answers a different question. A real system picks one answer from several families at the same time.

SWEBOK familyThe question it answersExamples · (the one StudyMate uses)
General structuresHow is the code organized into parts?Layered ✓ · pipes-and-filters · blackboard · monolith/microservices
Distributed systemsWhich machine talks to which, over a network?Client-Server ✓ · n-tier · REST · publish-subscribe
Method-drivenWhat programming paradigm drives it?Object-oriented · event-driven · data flow
User-computer interactionHow is the UI code kept apart from the logic?MVC ✓ · presentation-abstraction-control

So StudyMate is Layered (general structure) + Client-Server (distributed) + MVC (UI interaction) — three families, zero contradiction. The next four slides take one style at a time, one family at a time.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 3 · Style 1 · Layered
STEP 3 · STYLE 1 OF 4

Layered (N-Tier): stack responsibilities, top to bottom

The idea

Split the system into horizontal layers. Each layer only talks to the layer directly below it — the UI never touches the database directly.

Why teams pick it

Easy to reason about, easy to test one layer at a time, and it's the default shape most Year-1 projects should start with. (It's also low coupling by construction.)

Presentation
StudyMate's mobile screens — what the student taps
↓
Business logic
"Is this task overdue? Should a reminder fire?"
↓
Data
Where Student, Task, Course, Reminder actually live

Same three layers as your class diagram's entities (Student, Task, Course, Reminder) — the data layer is literally what you'll draw in Level 2.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 3 · Style 2 · Client-Server
STEP 3 · STYLE 2 OF 4

Client-Server: one side asks, one side answers

Split the system into two roles across a network: a client that asks for things and shows them to a person, and a server that holds the real data and answers every request the same way, for every client.

Where this is a layer, not the whole picture

Client-Server only describes WHO talks to WHO. It doesn't say how the server itself is organized inside — that's what Layered (previous slide) or Monolith/Microservices (next slide) describe.

Everyday examples

Web browser + website server. Mobile app + backend API. Even this slide deck's file + your laptop's browser (no server at all — worth noticing the contrast).

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 3 · Style 3 · MVC
STEP 3 · STYLE 3 OF 4 · SWEBOK "USER-COMPUTER INTERACTION" FAMILY

MVC: keep the screen, the decisions, and the data in three separate parts

MVC splits one app into three roles so that changing the look never breaks the logic, and changing the logic never breaks the look. It organizes code, not machines — a different question from Layered or Client-Server.

The three parts (in StudyMate)

Model = the data + rules (Task, Course, "is it overdue?"). View = the Today screen the student sees. Controller = catches a tap and decides what to do with it.

Follow one tap — the MVC loop

1. Student taps a task's checkbox → the View forwards the event to the Controller.
2. Controller calls task.markDone() on the Model.
3. The Model changes and notifies the View → the View re-draws that row as done. None of the three reaches into another's insides.

Common mix-up: Layered & Client-Server describe the system's physical shape (which machine, which layer). MVC organizes code inside one part — often inside the client, or inside the server. You use both at once: a layered, client-server app whose UI code is MVC. (Note the defining arrow most people forget: Model → View — the view reacts to the data, not to the controller.)

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 3 · Style 4 · Monolith vs Microservices
STEP 3 · STYLE 4 OF 4 · ONE DEPLOYABLE UNIT, OR MANY?

Monolith vs. Microservices

This is a question about how many separate things you deploy — one app that does everything, or many small apps that each do one job and talk to each other over the network.

MonolithMicroservices
ShapeOne deployable app — all features in one codebaseMany small, independently-deployable services
Best forSmall teams, one semester, one projectLarge teams, many independent squads, years of scale
Cost to runOne thing to deploy, test, debugNetwork calls between every service — much more to operate
Year-1 default✓ Start hereOnly if a real FR/NFR forces it

Microservices solve a team-scaling problem, not a code-quality problem. A 4-person student project almost never has that problem yet.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 4 · Record the decision
SWEBOK CALLS THIS "DESIGN RATIONALE" (§4.6) · A CHOICE + A REASON + THE ROADS NOT TAKEN

Write down WHY, not just what — and what you rejected

A design that only shows the final answer can't be defended. Record the alternatives you considered and why you chose one — three lines is enough. This is your oral-exam insurance.

QDecision: How does StudyMate know a task is due?
Chosen — check-on-open: compute what's due each time the app opens.
Alternative — daily background job: a server task runs at 8am and pushes reminders.
∴Why: our NFR is "one-semester, one server, no ops budget." Check-on-open needs no scheduler and no always-on worker. We'll revisit if users demand notifications while the app is closed.

The 3-line ADR (Architecture Decision Record)

1 · Context — what forces a choice (which FR/NFR).
2 · Decision — what we chose.
3 · Consequence — what we gain, what we give up, when we'd revisit.

Put it in M3 (your SDD)

One short ADR per real decision, inside your Software Design Description. SWEBOK's point exactly: record the alternatives and trade-offs, because next year's maintainer can't see the reasons in your head. Graders reward "we chose X because Y, over Z" far more than a slick diagram with no reasoning.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 1 · Read a class box
STEP 1 · BEFORE YOU DRAW · THE NOTATION, AND WHERE CLASSES COME FROM

How to read a class box — and how to find your classes

A class box asks two questions: what does it know? (attributes) and what can it do? (methods). A fast way to find them: in an FR sentence, the nouns become classes and the verbs become methods. "A student marks a task done" → classes Student, Task; method Task.markDone().

① Class name

One singular noun — the thing itself (Task, not "Tasks" or "TaskManager").

② Attributes — what it knows

Format visibility name: Type. Visibility: − private (hidden inside), + public, # protected. Default to − and expose through methods.

③ Operations — what it does

Format + name(params): ReturnType. These are the verbs from your FRs — the behaviour, not just storage.

Sanity check: if a box has attributes but no methods, it's just a data record — fine for a Model class, but ask whether the behaviour landed in the wrong place (a god-class doing everyone's job).

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 1 · Class design
STEP 1 · THE OBJECTS INSIDE THE BOX · NOW WITH RELATIONSHIPS

Class design: the classes, their data, and their jobs

A class diagram is three-compartment boxes — name · attributes · methods — joined by relationships. High cohesion means each class has one job; low coupling means few, clear lines between them. This is UML too, but the internal view (Week 4 was the external use-case view).

How to read it

+ public / − private. Each box is one class with one job. Task holds data + markDone(); TaskRepository stores/finds tasks; ReminderScheduler decides what's due.

The two relationships

Solid line + multiplicity (1 — *): one repository manages many tasks. Dashed arrow «use»: the scheduler depends on the repository — a dependency, not ownership.

Where it comes from

The nouns in your FRs become classes; the verbs become methods. "Remind me before a task is due" → Task, checkDue().

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 1 · The four lines
STEP 1 · READ ANY CLASS DIAGRAM · THE LINE IS THE MEANING

The four relationships you'll actually use

The line between two boxes carries meaning — get it wrong and the diagram lies. You'll draw the first two constantly; recognise the other two when reading a diagram.

ASSOCIATION · "has / knows"

Solid line + multiplicity. One course has many tasks. Both know each other; neither owns the other's life.

DEPENDENCY · "uses"

Dashed arrow. Scheduler calls the Repository to fetch, but doesn't store it. Weaker than association — a passing use.

GENERALIZATION · "is a kind of"

Hollow triangle points at the parent. A PushReminder is a Reminder. Inheritance — shared attributes/methods.

COMPOSITION · "part of / owns"

Filled diamond on the whole. Sections live and die with the Report. (Hollow diamond = aggregation, a looser "part of".)

Association + dependency cover ~90% of your needs. Reach for generalization only when classes truly share behaviour — don't invent inheritance to look clever (the AI will try).

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 2 · Data design
STEP 2 · WHERE THE DATA ACTUALLY LIVES

Data design: turn classes into tables (or documents)

Each class that must persist becomes a table. Attributes become columns with types; you add a primary key (PK, the unique id) and foreign keys (FK, the link to another table). Getting types and keys right now saves painful migrations later.

table · task
idINTEGERPK
titleTEXTnot null
due_dateDATEnullable
statusTEXT'todo' | 'done'
course_idINTEGERFK → course.id
table · course
idINTEGERPK
codeTEXT'CS101'
nameTEXTnot null

The course_id FK is the 1 — * line from your class diagram, made real: one course, many tasks.

No database? The same design applies to a JSON file or a document store — you still decide fields, types, and which id links to which. Pick types you can defend: why DATE not TEXT for a due date?

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 3 · Interface design
STEP 3 · THE CONTRACT BETWEEN MODULES

Interface design: what each part promises to the others

An interface is the small, stable set of calls a module exposes — the promise other modules rely on. Design it and you've defined low coupling: callers depend on the signature, never on the insides. If it's client-server, the same idea becomes your REST API.

// internal interface — what the Logic layer exposes interface TaskRepository { save(task: Task): void findDueBefore(date: Date): Task[] } interface ReminderScheduler { checkDue(now: Date): Task[] }
// if client-server: the REST contract GET /api/tasks?dueBefore=2026-09-01 → 200 [ Task ] POST /api/tasks { title, dueDate } → 201 Task PATCH /api/tasks/42 { status: "done" } → 200 Task

Design the interface before the implementation: two teammates can then build the caller and the callee in parallel, meeting only at this contract. That's abstraction paying off.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 4 · Behavior · Sequence
STEP 4 · HOW THE OBJECTS COLLABORATE OVER TIME

Sequence diagram: one scenario, message by message

A class diagram is a static snapshot. A sequence diagram shows one scenario play out — objects (top) exchanging messages down a timeline. Solid arrow = a call; dashed arrow = a return. Scenario: "student opens the app → sees today's reminders."

Draw a sequence diagram for your hardest FR — the one with real logic. It exposes missing methods and wrong ownership faster than any amount of staring at the class diagram.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · Step 4 · Sequence · Branches
STEP 4 · A SECOND SCENARIO · WHEN THERE'S AN "IF"

Show a decision with an alt frame

The first sequence assumed tasks were due. Real behaviour branches: what if nothing is due? A combined fragment labelled alt is UML's "if/else" — a box around the messages, split by a dashed line, with a [guard] condition on each half.

Legend: alt = a choice · [guard] = the condition for that half · a loop fragment (same box, labelled "loop") = repeat. Only draw a fragment when the branch matters — don't frame every message; the empty-state path above is exactly the kind your demo will be graded on remembering.

▶ intro2se-w5-design.fig · AI Critique #5
LAST 10 MINUTES · ON A4 · ONE SHEET PER PERSON

Spot it · Explain it · Fix it

"Put all the logic in one big class — it's simpler. And high coupling is good, because everything can reach everything it needs."

— an AI's design advice for your project
SPOT

What's wrong?

Two errors: one big class = low cohesion; "high coupling is good" is backwards.

EXPLAIN

Why does it matter?

A god-class is untestable and unownable; high coupling means one change breaks many modules.

FIX

What should it say?

Split into focused classes (high cohesion) that talk through small interfaces (low coupling).
